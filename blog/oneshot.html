How OneShot stayed with me forever

Ozersk, Russia — 07 Apr 2026 | Posted in Gaming

I hadn’t heard anything about this game for a long time. At most, I’d caught a passing mention here and there that it was “some kind of good indie game.” It sat gathering dust in my Steam backlog for quite a while until I finally got around to playing it recently.

There are two versions of the game, and I decided to go with the World Machine Edition - it’s newer, adapted for modern platforms, and comes with various nice extras. I launched it without any particular expectations and was immediately in for a shock.

Right from the start, the game made it clear: I’m not just an observer. As a real person sitting in front of the screen and interacting with system files, I’m a separate character in this world. For me, it was a completely new gaming experience, and it instantly won me over.

🖥️ Puzzles Beyond the Window

All the puzzles in the game are moderately challenging and very interesting (though, of course, once you know the solutions, they can be solved fairly quickly on a second playthrough). The World Machine Edition really comes into its own here: I actually had to dig through files on a virtual computer, inside which the code was hidden.

At one point, I had to drag files from one directory to another, using them as a kind of "portal," which directly affected what was happening in the game in real time. This interaction between the World Machine operating system and the OneShot game window itself within it absolutely thrilled me.

🎨 Light, Pixels, and Cosmic Music

Before discussing the plot, I want to highlight the atmosphere. The pixel art here is fairly simple, but that doesn't make the game any worse - quite the opposite. I'd also like to highlight the stunning lighting: the way soft gradients are applied to the pixel art adds depth to the locations and incredibly enhances the overall mood.

The atmosphere wouldn’t be complete without the soundtrack. The music in OneShot is out of this world. It sets the tone for the entire game: just the right amount of melancholy, perfectly conveying the feeling of a world fading away. Different locations sound different, and the soundtrack perfectly matches wherever we happen to be at that moment.

📖 A simple plot that hides an abyss

There premise seems simple enough: we must help a sweet child named Niko bring light back to a dying world. But the lore of this universe turns out to be truly vast.

There are so many well-developed characters here, and the setting tells stories just as effectively as the dialogue itself. Take, for example, the enigmatic figure of The Author, whose story is revealed bit by bit throughout the game and reaches its climax later on. We meet a ton of characters, and none of them feel like cardboard NPC extras. They stick in your memory, and you genuinely grow attached to them.

Speaking of characters, I can't help but mention my absolute favorite among the NPCs - the Shepherdess. In the game, you can stumble upon her hilarious Secret RAM Club (a brilliant play on words, given the computer-based nature of the world!). There, she delivers a whole series of funny jokes about sheep and rams.

A huge part of the charm lies in Niko himself. Every detail of his personality screams that he's just a kid. He gets sad, misses his mom, and genuinely loves the pancakes (oladushki) she used to make for him. He gets playfully offended when people call him a cat, and whenever he sees coffee, he keeps saying, "That's a drink for adults."

These heartfelt and lighthearted details make the game's world incredibly cozy, offering a respite between the tough moments. And it's precisely because of these moments that you start to genuinely love the inhabitants of this world - which will play a cruel trick on you in the future.

⚖️ The illusion of making the right choice

My first playthrough was coming to an end, and the game presented me with a brutal choice:

What to do: light the sun by sacrificing the main character (leaving him in this world forever), or shatter the lightbulb, condemning this world to darkness but allowing Niko to return home to his mother?

I sat in front of the screen and thought about it for a very long time. This choice was very difficult for me. In the end, I decided to save the world. To my immense relief, Niko himself agreed. It seemed like the story was over. But that was only the beginning.

⚠️ WARNING: Major spoilers for the ending (the "Solstice" mode) follow! If you haven't played the game yet, read at your own risk.

🌑 Solstice and the Collapse of the Simulation

Somehow, I managed to "hack" the system and start a new playthrough—the "Solstice" mode. And from the very first minutes, I got goosebumps. Starting a "new game," I realized that Niko already knew my name.

What's more, the Entity (the local AI that controls everything) also realized we were back. This is where the main twist is revealed, one that left me absolutely thrilled: this entire world is a simulation.

But then the real nightmare began. The story took a completely different turn. The Entity (the World Machine) lost control over us and triggered a process similar to virus removal or self-destruction. The world began to literally disintegrate before our eyes into pixelated squares, and along with it, the inhabitants and our friends began to perish.

This helplessness simply tore at my heart. As "Gods," there was nothing we could do to help them. The most painful moment came when the local aircraft ("flying machine") broke down due to a coding error. We simply didn't have time to save our friends from the impeding decay. That really hit home.

❤️ A True Farewell

Despite all the pain, this difficult journey led us to a happy ending. Before leaving this world forever, we were able to reunite with all our friends and say goodbye to them.

Niko really did come home.

What stuck me in particular was the developers' genius in how they justified the possibility of repllaying the game after the true ending. If I want to start the game again, the World Machine will honestly say:

"The real Niko is no longer here. But I can simulate him for you based on past records."

It's so well thought out and so considerate of the player's emotions!

📌 Conclusion

OneShot isn't just a game - it's an experience that blurs the lines between software and humanity. It has touched me deeply. I know that someday I'll definitely return to it. I'll wander through these locations again, see Niko again (even if only as a simulation), and once more illuminate this melancholic world with a new sun.
